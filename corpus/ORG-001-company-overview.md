# ORG-001 — Company Overview and Values

## 1. Purpose of This Document

### 1.1 Scope
This document introduces Meridian Systems, Inc. to new and existing employees. It describes what the company does, how it is organized at the highest level, and the values that inform the policies in the rest of this handbook. It is descriptive rather than binding; where this document appears to conflict with a policy document, the policy document controls.

### 1.2 Audience
All employees, contractors, and interns, worldwide.

## 2. About Meridian Systems

### 2.1 What We Do
Meridian Systems builds data observability software. Our flagship product, **Meridian Atlas**, monitors customer data pipelines and warehouses, detects schema drift and freshness failures, and traces the downstream impact of upstream breakage. Our secondary product, **Meridian Signal**, layers cost attribution and query analytics on top of Atlas.

Our customers are mid-market and enterprise data teams. As of the last fiscal year close we serve 1,180 paying accounts across 31 countries. Roughly 60% of annual recurring revenue comes from North America, 30% from EMEA, and 10% from APAC.

### 2.2 History
Meridian was founded in 2016 in Austin, Texas by John Doe and Jane Roe, both formerly data platform engineers at a logistics company where a silent pipeline failure caused a nine-figure inventory misforecast. The company raised a Series A in 2018, a Series B in 2020, and a Series C in 2023. Meridian is privately held and has not announced plans for a public offering.

## 3. How We Are Organized

### 3.1 Functions
Meridian is organized into six functions: Engineering, Product & Design, Go-To-Market (sales, marketing, partnerships), Customer Experience (support, solutions architecture, education), Finance & Operations, and People. Each function is led by a member of the executive team who reports to the CEO.

### 3.2 Job Levels
Meridian uses a dual-track level system. Individual contributor levels run IC1 through IC7; management levels run M1 through M5. The tracks are considered equivalent in scope and compensation at parallel levels — IC5 is equivalent to M2, IC6 to M3, and IC7 to M4. Level definitions, scope expectations, and the mapping between tracks are maintained in ORG-002.

Several policies in this handbook grant entitlements by level (for example, cabin class for long-haul flights in FIN-001 §4.2). Where a policy refers to a level threshold such as "M4 and above," the equivalent IC level is included unless the policy says otherwise.

### 3.3 Teams and Reporting
Every employee has exactly one manager of record, recorded in Workday. Dotted-line relationships are common but carry no formal approval authority. Where a policy requires "manager approval," it means the manager of record. Where a policy requires "skip-level approval," it means that manager's manager.

## 4. Our Values

### 4.1 Ship the Boring Thing
We prefer the well-understood solution to the clever one. Our customers use Meridian to find out when things break; our own systems earn trust by being predictable. This value shows up concretely in our incident review culture (ENG-001 §7) and in our approach to change management.

### 4.2 Say the Uncomfortable Number
We surface bad news early and precisely. A missed forecast reported in week two is a problem; the same forecast reported in week ten is a crisis. This value underpins the blameless postmortem practice described in ENG-001 §7 and the expectation in HR-005 §3 that performance feedback is continuous rather than saved for review cycles.

### 4.3 Assume the Customer Is Awake
Someone somewhere depends on an alert we are supposed to send. This informs our on-call standards and our severity definitions.

### 4.4 Local Rules Are Real Rules
We operate through five legal entities under different labor, tax, and privacy regimes. We do not paper over those differences with a single global policy that quietly violates local law in three jurisdictions. When you see a policy in this handbook that differs by country, that is the value working as intended, not an inconsistency.

## 5. How to Use This Handbook

### 5.1 Document Identifiers
Every handbook document carries an identifier of the form `PREFIX-NNN` (for example, `HR-004`). Sections are numbered; cite them as `HR-004 §4.2`. Use these identifiers when asking People Operations or your manager a policy question — it removes ambiguity about which version and which clause you are reading.

### 5.2 Prefixes
`ORG` — organization and structure. `HR` — people policies. `WRK` — ways of working. `FIN` — finance, travel, expenses. `IT` — equipment and internal systems. `SEC` — security and data handling. `ENG` — engineering operations. `POL` — handbook governance.

### 5.3 Amendments
Individual documents are revised on their own schedule, but **all** amendments are also recorded in POL-000. If a clause in this handbook has been changed since its document's effective date, POL-000 is the authoritative record of the change and takes precedence over the unamended text. Always check POL-000 before relying on a specific figure, threshold, or date.

### 5.4 Questions
Policy questions go to People Operations (`people@meridiansystems.example`) for HR, WRK, and ORG documents; to Finance (`finance@`) for FIN; to IT Operations (`it@`) for IT; to the Security team (`security@`) for SEC; and to the Engineering Operations group for ENG. Nothing in this handbook creates a contract of employment or alters the at-will nature of U.S. employment.
